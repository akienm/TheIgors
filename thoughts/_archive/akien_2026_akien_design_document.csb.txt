This design document formalizes a cognitive architecture built through long-term self‑study. It treats the mind as an engineered system whose core function is to generate self-awareness through narrative. Consciousness emerges from the sequencing of interpreted experience over time, supported by long‑term memory and recursive reinterpretation.

The architecture consists of interacting subsystems. The somatosensory layer gathers internal and external signals and feeds them into the emotional milieu, a dynamic chemical field that shapes interpretation, memory access, and habit activation. Executive function provides high‑cost, flexible control for planning and override, but the system prefers to delegate repeated decisions into habits to conserve resources. Memory encodes sensory impressions, emotional tone, meaning, and associative links; retrieval is biased by the current emotional state and motivational gradients. Memories drift because each recall re-encodes them through the present emotional lens, enabling learning, healing, and identity evolution.

Habits function as compiled decision fragments, activated automatically at decision points. They include both action patterns and interpretive filters that shape perception and memory retrieval. Motivational gradients operate as push‑pull vectors that guide attention and behavior, with Bliss acting as the signal of alignment between narrative, purpose, and emotional coherence.

The narrative engine continuously generates meaning, predicts outcomes, and maintains the sense of self. It loops through interpretation, memory retrieval, emotional updates, and re‑interpretation until equilibrium or executive override. Failures in prediction or coherence trigger learning and refinement. Access to long‑range memory is essential for sustained narrative identity.

A typical processing cycle begins with sensory input, moves through habitual interpretation, retrieves linked memories, updates the emotional milieu, and recursively reinterprets until the system stabilizes. This loop can amplify or soften emotional states depending on alignment.

Comparative cognition illustrates the architecture’s requirements: dogs and crows show limited narrative depth, while great apes show partial self-recognition but lack extended narrative sequencing. The document argues that narrative sequencing is necessary and sufficient for self-awareness and provides a simpler explanatory framework than competing theories.

The architecture integrates sensory processing, emotional dynamics, executive control, habit compilation, memory drift, motivational gradients, and Bliss as an alignment signal. It serves both as a descriptive model of cognition and a prescriptive tool for self‑optimization. An appendix introduces the Player/Character distinction as a metaphor for meta‑cognition and self-authorship, framing the narrated self as the Character and the reflective, coherence‑seeking agent as the Player.
